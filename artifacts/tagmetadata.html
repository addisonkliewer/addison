ScoopRoute Tagging and Metadata System

A fictional taxonomy and metadata framework showing how I organize documentation by product area, audience, platform, skill level, language, and content type to improve filtering, findability, maintenance, and personalization.

Artifact Brief

Key context for this sample

This artifact demonstrates the information architecture and governance work behind a large SaaS knowledge base.

The system separates customer facing tags from internal content metadata. Customer facing tags help readers filter documentation according to their role, product, experience level, language, and area of interest. Internal metadata helps writers track ownership, review dates, lifecycle status, release impact, source changes, analytics priorities, and subject matter expert validation.

The model is adapted to the fictional ScoopRoute product.

Artifact Story

Situation

As the SR knowledge base grew, customers needed a faster way to narrow hundreds of articles to the content most relevant to their role, product, industry, language, experience level, and current area of work.

The existing content organization had developed gradually across several years. Categories helped group articles, but inconsistent tagging could make filtering less useful. Freeform search keywords existed separately, and the system did not fully connect reader groups with search behavior or personalized content recommendations.

The documentation team also needed internal information for content maintenance. Writers needed to know which articles were current, who owned them, when they should be reviewed, why they had changed, and whether they had received technical validation.

Artifact Story

Task

I needed to create a controlled tagging model that supported customer navigation without turning every internal content attribute into a visible filter. The system needed to distinguish broad categories from individual tags, separate search keywords from controlled vocabulary, and define how writers should classify content that crossed roles, products, workflows, or interface areas.

I also needed to create governance rules so writers could propose new tags without creating duplicate, overly broad, or short-lived terminology.

Artifact Story

Action

I combined research from the Learning Experience team with product and content categories I had built over several years of documentation work, then organized customer-facing tags into major categories — Area, Industry, Language, Product Type, Role, Skill Level, and Content Type — so customers could narrow content by where they were working, which product they used, their role, and how much product knowledge they needed. I maintained the controlled vocabulary in Google Sheets and transferred approved values into Document360 for writers to apply, keeping freeform search keywords separate from the controlled taxonomy so keywords supported alternate terminology while tags supported consistent filtering.

I also added internal metadata for content ownership, lifecycle status, review dates, source of change, release impact, subject matter expert review, analytics priority, accessibility, and localization readiness, along with governance rules requiring writers to use approved values, apply the most specific accurate tags, avoid internal team names, and propose new tags through Documentation — approved only when a tag improved filtering, search, reporting, or maintenance, applied to several articles, represented a distinct concept, and didn't duplicate an existing value.

Artifact Story

Result

The system created a more consistent way to organize and retrieve documentation across a large knowledge base. Customers could filter content by product, role, experience level, language, and area of interest, while writers gained clearer rules for classifying articles and fewer opportunities to create duplicate or conflicting tags. The internal metadata also created a stronger foundation for content audits, release planning, review scheduling, localization, analytics, and ownership.

The model supported future personalization by making persona and product information more structured, and improved AI readiness by giving search and retrieval systems clearer signals about audience, subject, platform, and user intent. Most importantly, it treated findability as a content design problem rather than only a search problem — better classification helped customers reach more relevant documentation before they had to refine a search manually.

This sample was recreated for public use with fictional product terminology, generalized tag categories, and an expanded governance model — it does not include proprietary taxonomy exports, customer information, private analytics, or confidential platform configuration details.
